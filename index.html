Abstract

Robotic systems such as the da Vinci platform have changed minimally invasive surgery by giving surgeons tremor filtering,
motion scaling and three-dimensional vision. This article reviews three peer-reviewed sources to explain how surgical robots
work, the benefits they offer patients, and the physiological trade-offs involved. The evidence points to improved precision,
reduced blood loss and faster recovery. However, only a limited number of studies have demonstrated these benefits, and the
procedures carry costs, including reduced pulmonary compliance and possible limits on immune function.

Introduction

For centuries, surgery has depended on the steadiness and judgement of the human hand. Over the past two decades, however,
robotic systems have begun to extend what that hand can achieve. The da Vinci system, introduced in 2000, brought
tele-operated surgery and an enhanced three-dimensional view of the operating field into clinical practice (Mokhtari,
Hosseinzadeh and Nourazarian, 2024), and it remains the most widely used robotic surgical platform (Chauhan, Shinde and
Jain, 2023). This article explains how surgical robots work, what benefits they offer patients, and what trade-offs they
involve for patients. It draws on three peer-reviewed reviews, so its findings reflect summaries of existing studies rather
than new trials.

Analysis and discussion

How surgical robots work
Despite the name, surgical robots do not operate on their own. In the da Vinci design, the surgeon sits at a console while robotic arms hold and manipulate the instruments. A vision system provides a magnified view of the surgical site, and the surgeon stays in complete control of every action (Chauhan, Shinde and Jain, 2023).

The engineering value lies in how the system transforms the surgeon’s movements. Mokhtari, Hosseinzadeh and Nourazarian (2024) identify the elimination of tremor, scaled motion and three-dimensional visualisation as key advantages, meaning a large hand movement can become a tiny, steady instrument movement inside the patient. Giri and Sarkar (2012) add that the da Vinci system successfully addressed some important handicaps of conventional laparoscopic surgery, which is performed through small incisions using long, rigid instruments.

Benefits for patients
The main appeal of robotic surgery is that it allows complex operations to be performed in a minimally invasive way. Chauhan, Shinde and Jain (2023) report that robotic devices improve surgical accuracy, dexterity and visualisation, and link the approach to better postoperative quality of life. Giri and Sarkar (2012) similarly describe studies showing reduced blood loss and a lower percentage of postoperative complications, although only a limited number of studies have demonstrated these benefits.

At a physiological level, minimising tissue trauma reduces surgical stress and inflammation, which contributes to faster recovery. Robotic procedures also limit ischaemia–reperfusion injury and oxidative damage compared with open surgery (Mokhtari, Hosseinzadeh and Nourazarian, 2024). The technology is now used for procedures including prostatectomy, hysterectomy, nephrectomy and mitral valve repair (Mokhtari, Hosseinzadeh and Nourazarian, 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest the advantages are clearest in precision and short-term recovery, while long-term outcomes are less certain.

Physiological trade-offs
Robotic surgery is not free of drawbacks for the patient. Mokhtari, Hosseinzadeh and Nourazarian (2024) note that pneumoperitoneum (inflating the abdomen with gas) and steep head-down positioning can reduce pulmonary compliance and splanchnic perfusion while increasing hypercarbia. The same reduction in surgical stress that speeds recovery may also limit immune function (Mokhtari, Hosseinzadeh and Nourazarian, 2024).
